If you have ever stared at a shelf full of “bond repair” products and wondered why everything suddenly claims to fix your hair’s internal structure? Well, you are not alone. I researched so-called “bond repair” products for months to write this guide.

What I found out was that actual “bond repair” can possibly be done, but only during chemical processing (bleaching, some hair dyes, perms). The science is pretty thin, but it's potentially worth adding to your chemical services at the salon.

The rest of the “bond repair” products are just products formulated for damaged hair. And that puts me in a weird spot: these products genuinely can make damaged hair look and feel better, but the scientific claims don't hold up. And there's no evidence that they are any better than other products for damaged hair. 

**Updated Nov 6, 2026**: I rewrote this guide in response to Lab Muffin's post on one of the studies covered and what I've learned since then. 

## The Framework: Reactive Chemistry vs. Non-Reactive "Fiber Actives"

The easiest way to cut through bond-repair marketing comes from hair scientist Dr. Paul Cornwell (of TRI Princeton) <InlineReference id="194" />. When mapping how hair treatments actually work, he splits them along two axes: Internal vs. Surface (where the ingredient goes) and Reactive vs. Non-Reactive (whether it triggers a true chemical reaction):   

- Reactive (chemical reactions)
  - Internal: Chemical Treatments (bleach, perms, relaxers—and potentially covalent bond repair)   
  - Surface: "White space" (rarely used in haircare)   
- Non-Reactive
  - Internal: Fiber Actives (includes most at-home "bond builders," citric acid, proteins) 
  - Surface: Conditioners (silicones, cationic surfactants, oils)   

The entire "bond repair" marketing gimmick relies on implying they are doing something reactive when they are non-reactive. Brands sell you a non-reactive fiber active, something that penetrates the hair and temporarily reinforces it, while implying it’s performing a reactive chemical repair.

Why are hair companies doing this? IMHO it's because Fiber Actives are already extremely common in affordable products, so claiming to do something different provides a "science-y" marketing story to justify the high prices of many so-called bond repair products. 

## True Bond Repair (Salon Chemical Services Only)

Hair gets a lot of its strength from disulfide bonds, strong covalent bonds inside the hair that get snapped during chemical treatments like bleaching and perming.

A handful of patented actives specifically claim to intercept and reconnect these broken disulfide bonds:

- Olaplex no.1 : active Bis-Aminopropyl Diglycol Dimaleate
- Epres Professional Bond Repair: active Diethylhexyl Maleate
- k18 Pro Mist and Mask: active sh-Oligopeptide-78

Forming permanent covalent bonds puts you squarely in Cornwell's Reactive zone. That isn't like putting lotion on dry skin, it requires an actual chemical reaction under specific conditions (like high alkalinity or active hair-processing environments) that simply do not occur when you're doing a conditioning mask or scrunching in a styling gel.   

- Maleates (Olaplex, Epres): Need reactive targets that only exist during active chemical processing. Once hair is rinsed and finished processing, those broken ends are essentially "capped off" and can no longer react <InlineReference id="196" />. In fact, a 2020 spectroscopic study testing Olaplex No. 3 on already-bleached hair found zero increase in disulfide bonds at all, just a reshuffling of existing bonds and a smoother outer cuticle <InlineReference id="139" />.
- Peptides (K18): Work via a different mechanism,acting more like a molecular scaffold along disrupted keratin chains, which still relies on compromised or chemically opened hair and lacks strong independent proof of permanent covalent repair during routine wash days.

As the cosmetic chemists on The Beauty Brains podcast have noted, once a disulfide bond is broken, reversing that reaction requires extreme chemical modifications that simply aren't possible on a human head of hair, and no topical product can restore damaged hair back to virgin tensile strength <InlineReference id="139" />.

*There was a study that showed that potentially maleate-based actives were forming at least some type of covalent bonds outside of the chemical processing context, but since I wrote the original post, chemist Dr. Michelle Wong (popularly known as Lab Muffin) has written about methodological issues with that paper <InlineReference id="194" />.*

The verdict: Is true bond repair happening inside a bleach bowl? Maybe. Since bleaching is expensive and high-risk, I’d still ask a stylist to include a professional bond builder *during* the process.

## Non-Reactive "Fiber Actives" (Everything Else on the Shelf)

Once you leave the salon (or if you’ve never chemically processed your hair at all)you are out of the reactive zone. Any at-home mask, shampoo, conditioner, or curl cream claiming to permanently "repair broken disulfide bonds" in the shower is selling a story the science doesn't back up. This includes:   

- At-home bond masks (used outside of chemical processing)
- "Bond repair" shampoos, conditioners, gels, and leave-ins

That doesn't mean these products don't work! In Cornwell’s map, these are Internal Non-Reactive Fiber Actives (often paired with Surface Conditioners). They penetrate inside the damaged hair fiber to create temporary bonds (like ionic and hydrogen bonds) while smoothing down rough cuticle scales. If you have damaged hair this is very useful!

In my original post, I called this "weak" bond repair, which made it sound inferior. In reality, millions of temporary bonds working together can make damaged hair noticeably stronger, softer, and less frizzy.

But you don’t necessarily need expensive patented molecules to get temporary bonds. Common, inexpensive ingredients like citric acid do this all the time <InlineReference id="139" /> and AFK there is no evidence that more expensive actives perform better.

## Why People Swear These Products "Saved" Their Hair

If you read product reviews, you will find passionate testimonials from people saying a bond mask completely transformed their fried hair. Are they lying? No. 

When your hair is severely damaged, high-porosity, or snapping, filling it with effective non-reactive fiber actives and smoothing down the cuticle with conditioning agents makes a massive, visible difference. It gives hair slip, reduces friction, restores elasticity, and stops mechanical breakage.

Even those viral social media videos showing "before and after" microscope photos of smoother hair cuticles are real...they just prove the wrong thing. A flatter outer cuticle is evidence of great **surface conditioning**, not internal disulfide bond repair. You cannot see covalent bonds through a microscope lens; verifying actual internal structural repair requires molecular-level tools like **spectroscopy** (which, as we saw earlier, didn't find new disulfide bonds after at-home Olaplex use).

The disconnect isn't that the products don't work, it's the explanation for why they work. The marketing attributes those dramatic results to  permanent covalent bond reconstruction. In reality, you are getting phenomenal conditioning and temporary reinforcement. 


## What to actually do as someone with hair

Here's my take
- Getting bleach, a perm, or harsh dye? Ask your stylist to use a professional bond builder during the service—that’s the one window where reactive covalent repair might actually happen.   
- Shopping for wash-day products? Mentally translate “bond repair” on the label to “formulated for damaged hair.”

Under that paradigm you do not need to rush out an buy “bond repair” shampoo, conditioner, gel, hairspray, etc. just because your hair is damaged.  When I look at a product like the Olaplex gel, I consider it based on if it's a good gel, I don't actually give it bonus points for “bond repair” because it's definitely not doing that. 

Most importantly, don't let "bond repair" lull you into a false sense of security. Because these products aren't resetting your hair back to healthy, virgin strength, your hair is still damaged and cumulative damage still has consequences. I’ve heard way too many horror stories of people over-bleaching or over-processing their hair thinking they can "just use Olaplex" to undo it, right up until their hair breaks off. Treat damaged hair gently, space out harsh chemical services, and recognize that even the best fiber actives are managing damage, not erasing it.
