If you have ever stared at a shelf full of “bond repair” products and wondered why everything suddenly claims to fix your hair’s internal structure? Well, you are not alone. I researched so-called “bond repair” products for months to write this guide.

What I found out was that actual “bond repair” can possibly be done, but only during or after chemical processing (bleaching, some hair dyes, perms). The science is pretty thin, but it's potentially worth adding to your chemical services at the salon.

The rest of the “bond repair” products are just products formulated for damaged hair. There's a conundrum I'm in where on one hand these products really can help damaged hair, but on the other, I strongly dislike the shaky science of how some of them are marketed. 

**Updated Nov 6, 2026**: I rewrote this guide in response to Lab Muffin's post on one of the studies covered and what I've learned since then. 

## What Are Bond Repair Products?

I divide bond repair into two categories
- **Covalent bond repair**: Claims to actually get inside the hair and repair the specific covalent bonds broken during chemical processing (perming, bleaching, some hair color, relaxing, etc.). Examples of these are Olaplex and k18
- **Non-covalent bond repair**: Claims to help repair the hair by adding a bunch of temporary bonds. It doesn't repair bonds, but it can help!


## Potentially Actual Bond Repair

These are bond repair products are claim to target disulfide bonds, which are covalent bonds inside the hair that are commonly broken during chemical treatments (bleaching, perming, some hair dyes)

These include
- Olaplex no.1 : active Bis-Aminopropyl Diglycol Dimaleate
- Epres Professional Bond Repair: active Diethylhexyl Maleate
- k18 Pro Mist and Mask: active sh-Oligopeptide-78

These potentially actually could repair bonds, but based on everything I've read, only **during or shortly after the chemical treatment** <InlineReference id="139" /> <InlineReference id="194" />. Like if your stylist used Olaplex while highlighting your hair. 

There was a study that showed that potentially these actives were forming at least some type of covalent bonds outside of the chemical processing context, but since I wrote the original post, chemist Dr. Michelle Wong (popularly known as Lab Muffin) has written about methodological issues with that paper <InlineReference id="194" />. 

That doesn't mean these products don't work, it's just their science marketing story is a little sketchy. Especially when it comes to the actives added to everyday products like shampoo or hair gel. There's no way any literal bond repair is happening from like putting in a curl cream with these actives. 

So this category ONLY includes these actives used during chemical processing.

## Bonds and Repair Products (but not really bond repair)

This is everything else. Even things with the actives listed above, because at home in your shower they are not doing any kind of bond repair.

IMHO these are regular products formulated for damaged hair. Before the “bond repair” trend they would have been labeled something like "repair" or "restore." These products can be great for damaged hair and can have some useful actives. 

Dr. Paul Corwell of the Tri-Princeton Institute and Dr. Jennifer Marsh are two experts who have proposed defining bond repair as <InlineReference id="145" />. :
1. A product with ingredients that penetrate into the cortex
2. Improves or restores hairs internal structure
3. Enhances hair's mechanical properties such as improving its resistance to breakage

Some common actives that might fit this definition include: 

- **Acids**
	- Citric Acid <InlineReference id="142" />.
	- Gluconic Acid <InlineReference id="144" /><InlineReference id="147" />
- **Protein derivatives** which are a hugely diverse group of ingredients with varying levels of evidence behind them, and different properties based on their MW (molecular weight)<InlineReference id="143" />
	- hydrolyzed proteins like hydrolyzed keratin
	- peptides
	- amino acids like arginine, serine, and valine
- **Vitamins** like Panthenol<InlineReference id="141" /> 

If these sound familiar you're right! A lot of products labeled “bond repair” might have been labeled "protein treatment" or "damage repair" in the past. 

I used to call these "weak" bond repair but TBH that was just about the bonds and implied they were somehow worse. Lots of little temporary bonds can be very strong/effective, you'll just need to use these products regularly. 

## What to actually do as someone with hair

Here's my take
- If you're getting a chemical treatment like bleaching talk to your hairstylist about including a bond repair product, it's during/shortly after the process that these could potentially repair bonds
- Every time you see a hair product labeled “bond repair” at the store, mentally replace that with "formulated for damaged hair"

Under that paradigm you do not need to rush out an buy “bond repair” shampoo, conditioner, gel, hairspray, etc. just because your hair is damaged. 

When I look at a product like the Olaplex gel, I consider it based on if it's a good gel, I don't actually give it bonus points for “bond repair” because it's definitely not doing that. 

I list a lot of good products for damaged hair on my [high porosity](https://www.curlsbot.com/porosity/high-porosity) page. Some say “bond repair”, some don't. What matters is that they are made for damaged hair.
